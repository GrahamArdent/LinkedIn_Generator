## What was tested, in order
1. Rebuilt the SAME cybersecurity subject using reader → decision/tension → concept → evidence → telling/voice → review. Reused the Cloudflare and CERT-In incidents and office-key symbol. Compared its new engine/control outputs with the previous installed-trial pair.
2. Applied those same building blocks to the proposed AI question: when does AI save work versus leave work to checking? Used independent Stanford and METR research, including METR's newer methodological caution. Compared the AI pair with the rebuilt security pair.
3. Shuffled all six first drafts before a separate model audit. Revealed production identities only afterward for the interpretation below. Human judgments on the new drafts remain pending.

BUILDING_BLOCKS.json records the exact six decisions for each subject. They were assistant-prepared and frozen before generation, not represented as six separate model calls. Source-bundle editorial_building_blocks carries those decisions into the existing writer and baseline contexts. Both runs used the actual installed launcher and normal evidence admission, opportunity, draft, quality, at most one guarded rewrite, candidate selection and conditional visual-planning path. This is reuse of the engine, not a parallel writing implementation.

The new shared reader is managers and professionals approving work, with no specialist security/developer knowledge assumed. Graham remains individual author and Relatable Provocateur v1 remains the voice. The security thesis is a plain-language rendering of the same access-versus-authorization distinction; its consequence is framed around the reader's approval decision. The security image/mappings remain intact, with a new audience-oriented beat sequence. The AI telling is literal and compares two unlike research settings.

Audience, brief emphasis, explicit translation instructions and narrative beats changed together. This is an editorial process experiment, not an isolated causal ablation. Cross-subject evidence and telling also differ; it does not prove that any topic is universally more successful.

## Comparison 1: original security versus reframed security
The earlier pair used specialist phrases such as stolen API credentials, whitelisted infrastructure, fund-transfer APIs, mule accounts and transaction-stage validation. The new version uses direct requests between computers, recognized company systems and accounts receiving stolen funds. It connects the distinction to approving work early, rather than waiting for a technical payout-control discussion.

Concrete improvement: the reader is asked what proves that THIS request is permitted and what stops it when that evidence is missing. The same source facts still earn the point. The audit identifies translation from specialist language as the main improvement, not a changed opening or an equal word count.

Tradeoffs remain. Recognized company systems is less precise than the specific network allow-list mechanism. Product names such as Atlassian still add specialist baggage. The two incident summaries and repeated key explanation still occupy much of the post. The process improved accessibility; it did not eliminate source-summary structure or independently prove less human editing.

Within the new pair, engine B05 supplies scope/recipient/amount inspection detail; direct control B01 asks a clear blocking question and still leaves some jargon. These are assistant interpretations after the blinded audit, not a declared engine win.

## Comparison 2: rebuilt security versus AI transfer
Both now start from a decision a manager can recognize:
- Security: familiar access is not proof that this particular action has permission.
- AI: fast first output is not proof of less work through usable completion.

The AI drafts use an everyday output-versus-finished-work distinction rather than transplanting the office-key metaphor. They propose comparing similar tasks under a common quality standard and counting production, review and correction work. That proposal is explicitly separated from reported research findings.

The two studies do NOT establish that checking caused METR's slowdown, that the settings are matched, or that early-2025 tools describe current AI performance. The February2026 caution is retained. The question was allowed to survive the evidence without forcing the suggested checking explanation to become a factual conclusion.

Within the AI pair, engine B06 explicitly includes instruction time; direct control B02 makes comparable examples and the same quality standard particularly clear. Both still accumulate qualification paragraphs. Selection and measurement biases remains abstract language for a general audience. The engine's categorical time-saving sentence deserves softening: fast output alone does not ESTABLISH a saving, rather than proving there is none.

Transfer conclusion: reader/decision/concept/evidence/telling/review can serve both subjects while producing different explanations. The topic change is not required to make cybersecurity accessible. Nor does the AI topic by itself resolve repetition or evidence-heavy caveats.
