## B01

An office key can open the door. It cannot give you permission to take the equipment.

That distinction matters when approving work, too. A familiar account or company system is only part of the evidence.

Cloudflare reported that account access credentials stolen in the October 2023 Okta compromise were mistakenly considered unused and left unchanged. Attackers later used them to enter its Atlassian work environment.

The digital key still worked. That did not make the person using it entitled to enter.

A separate CERT-In advisory from October 2026 shows the distinction at the payment stage. Attackers used stolen access credentials to send direct computer-to-computer payment requests from the victims’ own recognized company systems. They bypassed application checks and moved funds to accounts controlled by attackers.

The requests came through accepted systems. The payments still lacked permission.

I’d bring that distinction into one practical review: choose a sensitive request you approve and trace what proves this particular action is permitted.

Can you connect it to an approved record covering the action, recipient and amount, where relevant? What stops it proceeding if that evidence is missing?

“Those are our account details” or “it came from our systems” leaves that question unanswered.

The office key is only an illustration. Digital access details can be copied and used remotely; a physical lock cannot capture the checks needed for a payment. These separate reports also do not prove that any particular safeguard would have prevented the incidents.

They do give managers a useful distinction to inspect: evidence that access works, and evidence that the requested action has approval.

## B02

Is the work done when AI produces something, or when you can actually use it?

If you approve other people’s work, that gap matters. A quick draft can still leave you with a long job.

The Stanford-published study of 5,172 customer-support agents reports an average 15% increase in issues resolved per hour with AI assistance. That measures resolved issues, which is closer to the finish line than output appearing.

In a different setting, METR’s randomized study found that 16 experienced open-source developers completing 246 tasks took 19% longer with early-2025 AI tools.

Different workers, tasks and tools. These are not a matched comparison, and the findings do not establish checking as the cause of the slowdown.

My takeaway: measure the work through to usable completion.

Pick one repeated task. Define what makes the result ready to use, then compare similar examples with and without AI. Count the time spent producing, checking and correcting the work, including the reviewer’s time. Apply the same quality standard to both.

That is a measurement I’d propose, not a result these studies reported.

Keep the evidence in its lane: the support finding concerns one deployment. METR’s early-2025 result is historical; selection and measurement biases mean its February 2026 follow-up does not provide a reliable current estimate.

For your next approval decision, ask how much work remains after the output arrives.

## B03

Your office key works. Does that give you permission to take the equipment home?

The lock has answered one question. Permission is another.

Cloudflare reports that credentials stolen in the October 2023 Okta compromise were mistakenly considered unused and left unrotated. Attackers later used them to access its Atlassian environment.

The key still worked. The lock accepted it.

That separate question of permission matters when money moves, too. CERT-In reports that attackers used stolen API credentials from victims’ own whitelisted infrastructure to call fund-transfer APIs directly, bypass application-level checks and send funds to mule accounts.

Accepted credentials. Allowed network origin. Unauthorized transfers.

The office-key image has limits: digital credentials can be copied and used remotely. A physical key does not capture shared network origins or transaction-stage validation. And taking equipment is an illustration, not an allegation about Cloudflare.

These are different accounts involving different systems. They illustrate the distinction between accepted access and authorized action; they do not prove that a particular defense caused different outcomes or would have prevented losses.

My practical takeaway: pick one sensitive action and inspect the point where it can actually proceed.

For a payout, what evidence of authorization must accompany an accepted credential or network path? Must a direct API call satisfy the required approvals and business checks, including an approved record, beneficiary validation and transaction limits?

Ask your team to show you what enforces those requirements at the final payment step.

A working key answers whether you can get in. Your payout controls still need to answer whether this money may move.

## B06

The draft arrives in seconds. Is the work done, or just ready for someone to check?

If you approve work, that distinction matters more than how quickly the text appears.

A Stanford-published study of 5,172 customer-support agents found that AI assistance increased issues resolved per hour by 15% on average. That measures resolved issues, a useful step beyond counting output.

A different setting produced a different result. In METR’s randomized study, 16 experienced open-source developers completing 246 tasks took 19% longer with early-2025 AI tools. These were experienced contributors working on familiar software projects.

My takeaway: a faster first draft is a promising start. It is not yet a time saving.

For one repeated task, I’d compare the time to usable completion with and without AI. Set the same standard for approval. Include the time spent giving instructions, checking the result and making corrections, including the approver’s time.

That is a measurement I’m proposing, not a result these studies reported. The practical test is whether AI reduces the total work needed to reach something you can use.

Keep the evidence boundary clear: the support finding concerns one deployment, and METR’s early-2025 result is historical. Its February 2026 follow-up had selection and measurement biases, so it does not supply a reliable estimate of today’s effect.

I’d judge the saving at the point of approval. The first output can arrive early while the work still finishes late.

Assess accessibility, usefulness, factual support, voice and required edits. Actual editing minutes remain unmeasured.
