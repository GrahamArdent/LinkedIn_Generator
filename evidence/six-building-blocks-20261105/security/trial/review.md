## Draft A

An office key can open the door. It cannot give you permission to take the equipment.

That distinction matters when approving work, too. A familiar account or company system is only part of the evidence.

Cloudflare reported that account access credentials stolen in the October 2023 Okta compromise were mistakenly considered unused and left unchanged. Attackers later used them to enter its Atlassian work environment.

The digital key still worked. That did not make the person using it entitled to enter.

A separate CERT-In advisory from October 2026 shows the distinction at the payment stage. Attackers used stolen access credentials to send direct computer-to-computer payment requests from the victims’ own recognized company systems. They bypassed application checks and moved funds to accounts controlled by attackers.

The requests came through accepted systems. The payments still lacked permission.

I’d bring that distinction into one practical review: choose a sensitive request you approve and trace what proves this particular action is permitted.

Can you connect it to an approved record covering the action, recipient and amount, where relevant? What stops it proceeding if that evidence is missing?

“Those are our account details” or “it came from our systems” leaves that question unanswered.

The office key is only an illustration. Digital access details can be copied and used remotely; a physical lock cannot capture the checks needed for a payment. These separate reports also do not prove that any particular safeguard would have prevented the incidents.

They do give managers a useful distinction to inspect: evidence that access works, and evidence that the requested action has approval.

## Draft B

An office key lets you open the door. It doesn’t give you permission to take the equipment home.

That distinction matters when approving work, too. A familiar account can make a request look legitimate before anyone checks what it is asking to do.

Cloudflare’s February 2024 incident account gives the key part a concrete meaning. It reports that account access details stolen in the October 2023 Okta compromise were mistakenly considered unused and left unchanged. Attackers later used them to enter its Atlassian work systems.

The lock still accepted the key.

A separate CERT-In report from October 2026 shows why permission for the particular action deserves its own check. Attackers used stolen access details to send fund-transfer requests directly between computer systems, from the victims’ own recognized company systems. Those requests bypassed application checks and moved money into accounts used to receive the stolen funds.

Recognized access. Unauthorized payments.

These are different reports about different systems. They illustrate the distinction between accepted access and permitted action; they don’t prove that any particular safeguard would have prevented either incident. And the key is only an illustration: digital access details can be copied and used remotely. No equipment theft is being alleged at Cloudflare.

My practical takeaway is to inspect one sensitive request you’re responsible for approving. Identify the evidence that permits that specific action: the approved record, its scope, the intended recipient and any applicable amount limit.

Then check whether that evidence must actually be verified before the action happens.

A familiar account or company tells you something about where a request appears to come from. Approval needs evidence about what it is allowed to do.
