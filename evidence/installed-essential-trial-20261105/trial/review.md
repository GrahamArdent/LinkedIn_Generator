## Draft A

A working office key opens the door. It doesn’t give you permission to take the equipment.

That distinction matters when we approve work, too. A familiar account or company can leave a crucial question unanswered: is this particular request permitted?

Cloudflare reports that account access details stolen in the October 2023 Okta compromise were mistakenly considered unused and left unchanged. Attackers later used them to enter its Atlassian work systems.

The key still worked. That was the problem.

A separate CERT-In report describes attackers using stolen access details to send payment requests directly between computers, from the victims’ own recognized company systems. Those requests bypassed application checks and moved money to accounts controlled by attackers.

Recognizing the company systems did not establish permission for those transfers.

The office key is only an illustration. Digital access details can be copied and used remotely, and a lock doesn’t capture how payment checks work. These different incidents support a distinction between accepted access and permitted action. They don’t prove that a particular safeguard would have prevented either incident.

My recommendation: pick one sensitive request you’re responsible for approving. Check what proves that the specific action is permitted, beyond the familiar account or company behind it.

For a payment, that means evidence of approval for this payment, including its amount and recipient.

If the explanation stops at “we recognize the account,” the permission question is still open.

## Draft B

An office key opens the door. It doesn’t give its holder permission to take the equipment.

That distinction is easy to see with a key. A familiar account or company name can make it easier to overlook.

Cloudflare reports that account access details stolen in the October 2023 Okta compromise were mistakenly thought to be unused and left unchanged. Attackers later used them to enter its Atlassian environment, a system for collaboration and managing work.

The digital key still worked.

In a separate account, India’s CERT-In reports that attackers used stolen access details to send direct computer-to-computer fund-transfer requests from the victims’ own recognized company systems. Those requests bypassed application checks and moved money into accounts used to receive the stolen funds.

A request coming from a recognized system did not make the transfer legitimate.

I read these accounts as illustrations of two separate questions: does the system accept this access, and is this particular action permitted?

They concern different organizations and systems. They don’t establish that any particular safeguard would have prevented the incidents. And the office key is only an illustration: digital access details can be copied and used remotely, while payment approval involves checks a door lock cannot represent.

My practical recommendation: take one sensitive request you’re responsible for approving and inspect what proves that specific action is allowed. Look for approval covering the actual action, such as its recipient, amount or scope.

If the explanation stops at “it’s a known account” or “it came from that company,” the permission question is still unanswered.

The key working is where the approval decision starts.
