# Graham: AI, trust and the black box

Two unedited first drafts from the installed engine drafting stage. Same evidence, author, audience and underlying question; only selected style changes. Neither is approved for publication. The earlier chat draft remains a separate positive reference.

## Version 1 — Concise Challenger

A convincing AI answer still needs a reason to rely on it.

In its March 2025 interpretability report, Anthropic traced parts of Claude 3.5 Haiku’s internal computations. Its method captured only a fraction of the computation, even on short prompts.

That is partial understanding. It doesn’t establish whether a particular answer is right or wrong.

I’d separate two questions: how did the model produce this answer, and what evidence supports using it?

For everyday work, the second question gives me something concrete to check. Does the cited source support the claim? Does the calculation hold up? Is a recommendation resting on an assumption I haven’t checked?

Those checks don’t reveal everything happening inside the model. They can still give an answer firmer footing than fluency alone.

I don’t need complete understanding to find AI useful. I do want the strength of the check to match the consequences of being wrong.

## Version 2 — Reflective Leader

I find it reasonable to trust a useful AI answer without understanding every step that produced it. The harder judgment is deciding what gives that trust a sound basis.

A clear answer can make a decision feel easier. I would still want evidence that it holds up under scrutiny, especially when someone else will bear the consequences.

Anthropic’s March 2025 interpretability report makes this tension concrete. Researchers traced parts of Claude 3.5 Haiku’s internal calculations, while acknowledging that their method captured only a fraction of the total computation, even on short prompts.

That is a limit on what the researchers could trace. It doesn’t establish that any particular answer is unreliable. Understanding how an answer was produced and checking whether it deserves reliance are related, but distinct, tasks.

Explanations can help with that checking, although their value depends on the circumstances. In a 199-participant experiment, Buçinca and colleagues found that interventions encouraging deliberate engagement reduced overreliance more than the simple explanations tested. Participants liked the most effective interventions less. Separately, five maze-task studies by Vasconcelos and colleagues found that explanations could reduce overreliance in some conditions. Neither study directly establishes how people use today’s workplace chatbots.

My practical conclusion is to make the checking proportionate to the consequences. For a draft, reviewing the wording may be enough. For a consequential recommendation, I would check the underlying sources and assumptions, and consider what could change the decision.

I don’t need complete access to a model’s inner workings to use its answers. I do need a reason for relying on them that I can defend.
