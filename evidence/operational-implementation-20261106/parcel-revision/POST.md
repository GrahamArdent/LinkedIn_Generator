A parcel can be packed quickly and still arrive unusable. Packing speed tells you little about the job until the contents reach someone in good condition.

I think AI’s first output is the packed box. A draft may appear ready, but someone still has to check whether it answers the request, correct errors, and finish what the recipient needs. If that work takes more effort than the first pass saved, faster output has not saved work.

The distinction matters because speed and quality can move differently. Stanford studied 5,172 customer support agents and found that AI assistance raised issues resolved per hour by 15% on average. Less experienced agents improved in both speed and quality. The most experienced saw small speed gains and small quality declines. That is a result from customer support, with meaningful differences across workers.

METR heard a related concern from developers: some said final code quality, tests, or documentation differed between work done with and without AI. METR also said its later productivity estimate was unreliable. Neither study shows that checking caused its results.

For me, the useful measure runs from the packed box through delivery. How much checking, correction, and completion does it take before someone can use the work? Count that whole task when deciding whether AI saves effort.
