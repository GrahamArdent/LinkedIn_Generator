## Draft A

When is a convincing AI answer enough to act on?

A clear explanation can make an answer easier to follow. That still leaves the question of whether it deserves our reliance.

In March 2025, Anthropic reported tracing parts of Claude 3.5 Haiku’s internal computations. Its method captured only a fraction of the computation, even on short prompts.

Partial understanding still gives us something to work with. But an answer’s reliability and our access to how it was produced are separate questions.

There is also a tradeoff in checking answers. In a 199-participant study by Buçinca and colleagues, interventions encouraging deliberate engagement reduced overreliance more than the simple explanation approaches tested. Participants rated the most effective interventions less favorably.

That experiment predates current chatbots. It cannot settle how professionals should use them today. And explanations can help: separate controlled maze-task studies found reduced overreliance in some conditions.

My takeaway: I would judge an AI answer by what supports it, including evidence I can check and the consequences of being wrong.

For everyday work, that means deciding what needs verification before letting fluency settle the decision.

Before acting on the next convincing answer, what would you need to check?

## Draft B

What makes an AI answer worth relying on?

A convincing answer and a sound reason to trust it are different things. Asking for an explanation can produce more words without closing that gap.

In March 2025, Anthropic reported tracing parts of Claude 3.5 Haiku’s internal calculations. Its method captured only a fraction of the total computation, even on short prompts.

Partial understanding doesn’t establish that a particular answer is wrong. Nor does it make the model’s explanation a complete account of how it reached that answer.

There’s a separate question: does an explanation help someone judge the answer?

In a 199-participant study by Buçinca and colleagues, interventions prompting deliberate thought reduced overreliance more than the simple explanations tested. Participants rated the most effective interventions less favorably.

That experiment predates current chatbots. Explanations can help in some conditions. Still, I take the finding as a reason to distinguish assistance that feels helpful from assistance that helps me judge.

My standard would be evidence suited to the decision: check the cited source, test the calculation, examine the assumption that could change the conclusion. The greater the consequence of an error, the stronger the check should be.

I don’t need a complete account of an AI’s internal workings to use its answer. I do need a reason to rely on it beyond how well it explains itself.
